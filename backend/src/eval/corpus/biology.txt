Cell Biology Study Notes

All living organisms are composed of cells, the smallest units capable of independent life. The cell theory states three things: all organisms are made of one or more cells, the cell is the basic unit of structure and organization in organisms, and all cells arise from pre-existing cells through division. This theory was developed in the 1830s and 1840s based on the microscope work of Schleiden, Schwann, and Virchow, and it remains one of the foundational ideas of modern biology.

Cells are broadly divided into two categories: prokaryotic and eukaryotic. Prokaryotic cells, found in bacteria and archaea, lack a membrane-bound nucleus and most other membrane-bound organelles. Their genetic material floats freely in a region called the nucleoid. Eukaryotic cells, found in plants, animals, fungi, and protists, contain a true nucleus enclosed by a nuclear envelope, along with a variety of specialized organelles that carry out distinct functions. Eukaryotic cells are generally larger and more structurally complex than prokaryotic cells.

The nucleus is often called the control center of the eukaryotic cell because it houses the cell's DNA in the form of chromatin, which condenses into chromosomes during cell division. The nucleus is surrounded by a double membrane called the nuclear envelope, which is perforated by nuclear pores that regulate the movement of molecules like RNA and proteins into and out of the nucleus. Inside the nucleus, a dense region called the nucleolus is responsible for assembling ribosomal subunits.

Mitochondria are membrane-bound organelles often described as the powerhouse of the cell because they generate most of the cell's supply of adenosine triphosphate, or ATP, through a process called cellular respiration. Mitochondria have their own small circular genome and double membranes, an inner membrane folded into structures called cristae to increase surface area for chemical reactions. This unusual structure supports the endosymbiotic theory, which proposes that mitochondria originated as free-living bacteria that were engulfed by an ancestral eukaryotic cell.

The endoplasmic reticulum, or ER, is a network of membranous tubules and sacs that extends throughout the cytoplasm. Rough ER is studded with ribosomes on its outer surface and is heavily involved in the synthesis and folding of proteins destined for secretion or for insertion into membranes. Smooth ER lacks ribosomes and instead specializes in lipid synthesis, detoxification of drugs and poisons, and storage of calcium ions, particularly in muscle cells where it is called the sarcoplasmic reticulum.

The Golgi apparatus works closely with the endoplasmic reticulum, receiving proteins and lipids in transport vesicles, then modifying, sorting, and packaging them for delivery to their final destinations, whether that is another organelle, the plasma membrane, or outside the cell entirely. The Golgi is organized into stacked, flattened sacs called cisternae, with a distinct receiving face called the cis face and a shipping face called the trans face.

Lysosomes are membrane-bound organelles filled with digestive enzymes that break down worn-out organelles, food particles, and engulfed viruses or bacteria. They function at an acidic internal pH that activates their hydrolytic enzymes while protecting the rest of the cell from damage if a lysosome happens to leak. Cells that carry out extensive digestion, such as white blood cells that engulf pathogens, tend to be especially rich in lysosomes.

The cell membrane, or plasma membrane, forms the boundary of the cell and controls what enters and exits. It is composed of a phospholipid bilayer in which hydrophilic heads face outward toward the watery environment on both sides, while hydrophobic tails face inward, away from water. Embedded within this bilayer are proteins that serve as channels, pumps, receptors, and identification markers, giving rise to the widely used fluid mosaic model of membrane structure.

Photosynthesis takes place inside chloroplasts, organelles found in plant cells and algae that convert light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose. Chloroplasts contain a green pigment called chlorophyll, housed in internal membrane stacks called thylakoids, which are themselves organized into stacks called grana. The fluid surrounding the grana, called the stroma, is where carbon dioxide is fixed into sugar during the Calvin cycle. Like mitochondria, chloroplasts have their own DNA and are believed to have originated from engulfed photosynthetic bacteria.

The cytoskeleton is an internal scaffolding of protein filaments that gives the cell its shape, allows it to move, and provides tracks for transporting organelles within the cytoplasm. It consists of three main components: microfilaments, made of actin and important for muscle contraction and cell division; intermediate filaments, which provide mechanical strength; and microtubules, hollow tubes made of tubulin that form the mitotic spindle during cell division and make up the core of cilia and flagella.

Cell division in eukaryotes occurs through mitosis, a process divided into distinct phases. During prophase, chromatin condenses into visible chromosomes and the mitotic spindle begins to form. During metaphase, chromosomes align along the cell's equatorial plane. During anaphase, sister chromatids are pulled apart toward opposite poles of the cell. During telophase, two new nuclear envelopes form around the separated chromosome sets, and the cell proceeds to cytokinesis, where the cytoplasm physically divides to produce two daughter cells.

Diffusion and osmosis describe how substances move across the cell membrane without expending energy. Diffusion is the net movement of particles from an area of high concentration to an area of low concentration, driven purely by random molecular motion. Osmosis is a special case of diffusion referring specifically to the movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane, moving toward the side with higher solute concentration until equilibrium is reached, unless the cell actively intervenes.

Active transport, unlike diffusion, requires the cell to expend energy in the form of ATP to move substances against their concentration gradient, from low to high concentration. The sodium-potassium pump is a classic example, using ATP to move three sodium ions out of the cell for every two potassium ions moved in, a process essential for maintaining the electrical gradient needed for nerve impulses in animal cells.
