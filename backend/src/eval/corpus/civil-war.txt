The American Civil War: Study Notes

The American Civil War was fought from 1861 to 1865 between the United States, commonly called the Union, and eleven Southern states that seceded to form the Confederate States of America. The war remains the deadliest conflict in American history, with estimates of around 620,000 to 750,000 soldier deaths, and it fundamentally reshaped the nation's political, economic, and social structure.

The root cause of the war was the dispute over slavery, particularly whether it would be permitted to expand into the new territories being added to the United States as the country grew westward. Southern states depended economically on enslaved labor to sustain their plantation agriculture, especially cotton production, while a growing abolitionist movement in the North viewed slavery as a moral evil that had to be contained or ended. Attempts at compromise, such as the Missouri Compromise of 1820 and the Compromise of 1850, only temporarily delayed the underlying conflict.

The election of Abraham Lincoln in November 1860 served as the immediate trigger for secession. Although Lincoln did not campaign on abolishing slavery where it already existed, Southern leaders feared his Republican Party would block slavery's expansion and eventually threaten the institution entirely. South Carolina was the first state to secede in December 1860, followed quickly by Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, and Texas, which together formed the Confederate States of America in February 1861 under President Jefferson Davis.

The war formally began on April 12, 1861, when Confederate forces under General P.G.T. Beauregard opened fire on Fort Sumter, a Union-held fort in the harbor of Charleston, South Carolina. The fort surrendered the following day. In response, Lincoln called for 75,000 volunteer troops to suppress the rebellion, a move that prompted four more states, Virginia, Arkansas, Tennessee, and North Carolina, to secede and join the Confederacy rather than supply troops against fellow Southerners.

The Union held significant material advantages throughout the war, including a much larger population of about 22 million compared to the Confederacy's 9 million, of whom nearly 4 million were enslaved. The North also possessed the vast majority of the nation's industrial capacity, railroads, and financial resources, allowing it to produce weapons, uniforms, and supplies at a scale the agrarian South could never match. The Confederacy's main advantages were fighting a defensive war on familiar terrain and having many experienced military officers who had resigned from the U.S. Army.

Early in the war, Confederate forces won several notable victories in the Eastern theater, including the First and Second Battles of Bull Run, largely due to strong leadership from generals like Robert E. Lee and Stonewall Jackson. These early defeats damaged Union morale and led Lincoln to search repeatedly for generals capable of matching Lee's aggressiveness and tactical skill on the battlefield.

The Battle of Antietam, fought on September 17, 1862, near Sharpsburg, Maryland, remains the single bloodiest day in American military history, with roughly 22,000 combined casualties. Although tactically inconclusive, the battle halted Lee's first invasion of the North and gave Lincoln the political opening he needed to issue the preliminary Emancipation Proclamation, announcing that enslaved people in Confederate-held territory would be declared free starting January 1, 1863.

The Emancipation Proclamation transformed the war's purpose from simply preserving the Union into a fight that also targeted the abolition of slavery. It authorized the enlistment of Black soldiers into the Union Army, and by the war's end nearly 200,000 Black men, most of them formerly enslaved, had served in the Union Army and Navy. The proclamation also discouraged European powers like Britain and France, which had already abolished slavery, from recognizing or aiding the Confederacy.

The Battle of Gettysburg, fought July 1 to 3, 1863, in Pennsylvania, is widely regarded as the turning point of the war in the Eastern theater. Lee's second invasion of the North ended in a costly defeat, culminating in the disastrous Confederate assault known as Pickett's Charge on the third day. Union forces under General George Meade suffered roughly 23,000 casualties and Confederate forces roughly 28,000, and Lee's army never again mounted a major offensive into Northern territory.

At almost the same time, in the Western theater, Union General Ulysses S. Grant achieved a decisive victory at the Siege of Vicksburg, which surrendered on July 4, 1863, giving the Union complete control of the Mississippi River and splitting the Confederacy in two. Grant's success in the West led Lincoln to eventually promote him to command all Union armies in 1864, after which Grant adopted a strategy of relentless pressure against Lee's forces in Virginia.

General William Tecumseh Sherman's March to the Sea in late 1864 embodied the Union's new strategy of total war, deliberately targeting Southern infrastructure, railroads, and civilian resources to destroy the Confederacy's capacity and will to keep fighting. Sherman's forces captured Atlanta in September 1864, a victory that significantly boosted Lincoln's chances of reelection that November, and then marched to Savannah, leaving a trail of destruction roughly sixty miles wide.

The war effectively ended when General Robert E. Lee surrendered the Army of Northern Virginia to General Ulysses S. Grant at Appomattox Court House on April 9, 1865. Grant offered generous terms, allowing Confederate soldiers to return home without punishment as long as they laid down their arms. Remaining Confederate forces surrendered over the following weeks, formally concluding the rebellion by the summer of 1865.

President Lincoln did not live to see the full peace, as he was assassinated by John Wilkes Booth on April 14, 1865, just five days after Lee's surrender, while attending a play at Ford's Theatre in Washington, D.C. His death shocked the nation and complicated the already difficult process of Reconstruction, the effort to reintegrate the former Confederate states and guarantee the rights of newly freed African Americans.

The war's most lasting legal legacy came through three constitutional amendments passed shortly after its end. The Thirteenth Amendment, ratified in December 1865, abolished slavery throughout the United States. The Fourteenth Amendment, ratified in 1868, granted citizenship and equal protection under the law to all persons born or naturalized in the country, including former slaves. The Fifteenth Amendment, ratified in 1870, prohibited denying the right to vote based on race, though in practice many Southern states found ways to suppress Black voting for decades afterward.
